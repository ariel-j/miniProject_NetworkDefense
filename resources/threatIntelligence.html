🧠 Understanding Modern Threat Intelligence

Threat Intelligence (TI) is evidence-based knowledge about existing or emerging threats that helps organizations make informed security decisions. In 2024, social engineering, cloud intrusions, and malware-free techniques surged, and nation-state actors intensified cyber espionage and added AI to their arsenal.

Current Threat Landscape 2024-2025:

  • Manufacturing Overtakes Technology: Manufacturing has now overtaken technology as the most targeted sector, as we see a continued focus from adversaries on targeting supply chain critical infrastructure
  • AI-Enhanced Attacks: 67.4% of 2024 attacks use AI-enhanced techniques, requiring specialized intelligence approaches
  • Credential Abuse: Abusing valid accounts remained the preferred entry point into victim environments for cybercriminals in 2024, representing 30% of all incidents
  • Nation-State Evolution: Microsoft tracks more than 1,500 unique threat groups—including more than 600 nation-state threat actor groups

Understanding these evolving threats requires structured intelligence processes that can adapt to the rapidly changing cyber landscape while providing actionable insights for defense strategies.

🔄 The Threat Intelligence Lifecycle

The threat intelligence lifecycle is a continuous process that involves the collection, analysis, and application of data related to cyber threats. This systematic approach ensures organizations can proactively defend against evolving threats.

1

Planning & Direction

Define intelligence requirements and objectives

🎯
Organizations need to understand what awaits them in the following stages. They need to ask – who will benefit from the finished intelligence? Is it an analyst team, or the executive board?

Key Activities:

  • Identify Priority Intelligence Requirements (PIRs)
  • Define target audience and stakeholders
  • Establish collection priorities
  • Determine resource allocation
  • Set timelines and deliverables

Essential Questions:

  • What threats are we most concerned about?
  • Who needs this intelligence and in what format?
  • What are our critical assets and vulnerabilities?
  • How will success be measured?
2

Collection

Gather raw intelligence from multiple sources

📡
Systematic gathering of relevant threat data from internal and external sources to address defined intelligence requirements.

Intelligence Sources:

🌐 Open Source Intelligence (OSINT)
  • Public threat reports
  • Security blogs and forums
  • Social media monitoring
  • Government advisories
🏢 Internal Sources
  • Security logs and events
  • Incident response data
  • Vulnerability scans
  • Network monitoring
🤝 Commercial & Shared
  • Threat intelligence feeds
  • Industry sharing groups
  • Vendor reports
  • STIX/TAXII feeds
🕵️ Human Intelligence (HUMINT)
  • Dark web monitoring
  • Underground forums
  • Threat actor communications
  • Expert interviews
3

Processing

Transform raw data into structured formats

⚙️
Convert raw intelligence into structured, machine-readable formats that enable efficient analysis and correlation.

Processing Activities:

  • Data normalization and standardization
  • Format conversion (STIX/TAXII)
  • Duplicate removal and deduplication
  • Data enrichment and contextualization
  • Quality assurance and validation

🔗 STIX/TAXII Standards:

STIX states the "what" of threat intelligence, while TAXII defines "how" that information is relayed. Unlike previous methods of sharing, STIX and TAXII are machine-readable and therefore easily automated.

4

Analysis & Production

Create actionable intelligence and insights

🔬
The analysis phase is a largely qualitative and often human-oriented process aimed at contextualizing processed threat intelligence through the enrichment and application of known structural data or advanced correlation and data modeling.

Analysis Types:

📊 Strategic Intelligence

High-level threat landscape assessments for executive decision-making

⚡ Tactical Intelligence

Specific IOCs and TTPs for immediate defensive actions

🎯 Operational Intelligence

Campaign analysis and threat actor attribution

🔧 Technical Intelligence

Detailed malware analysis and infrastructure mapping

5

Dissemination

Deliver intelligence to relevant stakeholders

📤
The final stage is the dissemination of the finished threat intelligence, and it involves getting the intel to the targeted audience through secure channels. Proper dissemination ensures the right information reaches the right people at the right time.

Dissemination Methods:

  • Automated feeds to security tools
  • Executive briefings and dashboards
  • Technical reports for SOC teams
  • Threat bulletins and alerts
  • Industry sharing platforms
6

Feedback

Evaluate effectiveness and improve processes

🔄
Feedback will boost the outcome of the next iteration of the cycle. It will also improve your security operations, as well as the overall security strategy of the organization.

Key Metrics:

  • Intelligence utilization rates
  • Time to detection improvement
  • False positive reduction
  • Stakeholder satisfaction
  • Process efficiency gains

🏔️ The Pyramid of Pain

The Pyramid of Pain is a conceptual framework in cybersecurity that illustrates the different levels of indicators of compromise (IoCs), ranking them from the easiest to the most painful for adversaries to change. Understanding this framework helps prioritize threat intelligence efforts for maximum impact.

🎯

TTPs (Tactics, Techniques, Procedures)

Extreme Pain

Behavioral patterns and methodologies used by threat actors. TTPs illustrate the broader attack picture and how it connects to other stages of an attack or campaign.

Examples: Spear phishing techniques, lateral movement patterns, persistence mechanisms
🛠️

Tools

High Pain

Software and utilities used by attackers. Changing tools requires significant effort and testing.

Examples: Cobalt Strike, Mimikatz, custom malware families
🖥️

Network/Host Artifacts

Medium Pain

Observable modifications to systems or network configurations.

Examples: Registry keys, file paths, process names, network patterns
🌐

Domain Names

Low-Medium Pain

DNS domains used for command and control or malicious hosting.

Examples: C2 domains, phishing sites, malware download locations
📍

IP Addresses

Low Pain

Network addresses used in attacks. Easily changed through proxies or hosting services.

Examples: C2 servers, scanning sources, malicious hosting IPs
#️⃣

Hash Values

Trivial Pain

Hash values of malware binaries or tools, once detected and shared, are perceived as trivial to modify for an adversary.

Examples: MD5, SHA1, SHA256 file hashes

🔗 STIX/TAXII Standards

STIX and TAXII are standards developed to improve the prevention and mitigation of cyber threats and attacks. These frameworks enable automated, standardized sharing of threat intelligence across organizations and platforms.

📋

STIX (Structured Threat Information eXpression)

STIX is a standardized language that uses a JSON-based lexicon to express and share threat intelligence information in a readable and consistent format.

Core STIX Objects:

🎯 Attack Pattern: Describes techniques used by adversaries
🏃 Campaign: Grouping of adversarial behaviors targeting specific objectives
📊 Indicator: Contains patterns for detecting suspicious or malicious activity
🦠 Malware: Represents malicious code or software
👤 Threat Actor: Individuals or groups conducting malicious activities
🛠️ Tool: Legitimate software misused for malicious purposes
🚚

TAXII (Trusted Automated eXchange of Intelligence Information)

TAXII serves as the dedicated transport mechanism for sharing this information. TAXII complements STIX's structured language by facilitating automated and secure exchange of STIX content across different networks and platforms.

TAXII Services:

🔍 Discovery: Locate available TAXII services and collections
📚 Collection Management: Manage data collections and subscriptions
📥 Inbox: Receive threat intelligence content (push)
📤 Poll: Request threat intelligence content (pull)

📋 Types of Threat Intelligence

🏛️

Strategic Intelligence

Executives & Leadership
High-level intelligence focusing on long-term trends, geopolitical factors, and business risks.

Characteristics:

  • Long-term perspective (6-12 months)
  • Business impact focused
  • Non-technical language
  • Risk-based assessments
Examples: Industry threat reports, geopolitical risk assessments, regulatory compliance impacts
⚡

Operational Intelligence

Security Managers
Campaign-focused intelligence providing context about ongoing threats and adversary activities.

Characteristics:

  • Medium-term focus (weeks to months)
  • Campaign attribution
  • Threat actor profiling
  • Attack pattern analysis
Examples: APT campaign analysis, threat actor TTPs, attack timeline reconstruction
🎯

Tactical Intelligence

SOC Analysts
Immediate, actionable intelligence with specific IOCs and defensive measures.

Characteristics:

  • Short-term relevance (hours to days)
  • Machine-readable format
  • Automated integration
  • High-confidence indicators
Examples: IP blacklists, malware signatures, IOC feeds, YARA rules
🔬

Technical Intelligence

Malware Analysts
Detailed technical analysis of malware, vulnerabilities, and attack infrastructure.

Characteristics:

  • Deep technical detail
  • Reverse engineering focus
  • Infrastructure mapping
  • Vulnerability analysis
Examples: Malware analysis reports, exploit code analysis, infrastructure mapping

🗂️ Key Threat Intelligence Frameworks

⚔️

MITRE ATT&CK

A globally-accessible knowledge base of adversary tactics and techniques based on real-world observations.

Key Features:

  • 14 tactical categories
  • 200+ techniques
  • 400+ sub-techniques
  • Threat actor mapping
🔗

Cyber Kill Chain

A model describing the stages of a cyberattack from initial reconnaissance to achieving objectives.

Seven Stages:

  • Reconnaissance
  • Weaponization
  • Delivery
  • Exploitation
  • Installation
  • Command & Control
  • Actions on Objectives
💎

Diamond Model

A framework focusing on the relationships between four key elements of every intrusion event.

Four Elements:

  • Adversary (who)
  • Capability (what)
  • Infrastructure (how)
  • Victim (whom)
🌐

OWASP Threat Dragon

A threat modeling tool that helps identify and mitigate potential security threats.

Applications:

  • Threat modeling
  • Risk assessment
  • Security design
  • Vulnerability analysis

🚀 Building Your Threat Intelligence Program

Phase 1: Foundation (Months 1-3)

  • Establish intelligence requirements and stakeholder needs
  • Set up basic collection capabilities (OSINT, feeds)
  • Implement STIX/TAXII infrastructure
  • Train initial analyst team

Phase 2: Enhancement (Months 4-6)

  • Integrate with security tools (SIEM, EDR)
  • Develop analysis processes and workflows
  • Establish dissemination channels
  • Create feedback mechanisms

Phase 3: Maturation (Months 7-12)

  • Advanced analytics and threat hunting
  • Industry collaboration and sharing
  • Automated processing and enrichment
  • Continuous improvement based on feedback
